Estar: how things are right now

estar-for-state · estar-for-location · ser-vs-estar

You walk into a café in Salamanca at nine in the morning. The waiter looks up and says, «¿Cómo está?» — and then, an hour later, as you are leaving: «El café está frío.» Same verb, twice, and neither time is he telling you what something is.

Estar is the verb Spanish uses for conditions and locations — the things that could be otherwise. The coffee is cold now; it was hot twenty minutes ago. You are tired today; yesterday you were not. Ser, which you met in the last lesson, does the opposite work.

The pairing that trips up almost every English speaker is estar bueno versus ser bueno. «La sopa es buena» says the soup is a good soup — well made, a good recipe. «La sopa está buena» says the soup tastes good right now, in this bowl, in front of you.

Checkpoint

A friend hands you a bowl of soup, you taste it, and it is delicious. Which do you say?

estar bueno versus ser bueno

«Ser bueno» describes a quality the thing has in itself — a good film, a kind person, a well-made soup. «Estar bueno» describes how it strikes you at this moment.

Concretely: a restaurant critic writing a review reaches for es buena. You, tasting the first spoonful at the table, reach for está buena.

One caution worth carrying: in much of Spain, «estar bueno» said of a person is slang for attractive. Keep it to food and objects until you have a feel for the room.
